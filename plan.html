ILLUSTRATIVE 2030–2040 ECONOMIC MODEL

When We Win! Project Democratic Socialism: 10 Year Plan for America.

2030–2040

This section brings Boris Yagudayev's stated economic and social-policy proposals together into one ten-year scenario. The title reflects Yagudayev's framing; it is not an election forecast. The figures below are illustrative modeling assumptions, not an official government score, and should not be read as promises of specific economic outcomes.

THE IDEA

A larger social guarantee paired with a larger productive economy

The model asks what could happen if the United States combined universal healthcare, child care and pre-K, tuition-free public higher education and technical training, a stronger Social Security floor, a modest universal income, an employment backstop, large-scale housing and infrastructure investment, stronger worker participation and a gradual move toward a shorter workweek.

It assumes these programs are financed substantially through dedicated healthcare and Social Security contributions, more progressive individual and corporate taxation, taxation of capital income, savings from a phased reduction in defense spending and new revenue connected to the AI economy.

The central economic bet is that artificial intelligence and automation substantially raise American productivity during the 2030s. Rather than assuming that all of those gains remain private, the model combines private innovation with an AI-related public wealth fund and public revenue mechanisms so that part of the productivity gain can support broader public benefits and investment.

The model does not assume that federal spending itself creates unlimited resources. Housing, healthcare capacity, energy, infrastructure, skilled labor and productivity must expand alongside purchasing power to limit inflationary pressure.

WHAT THE PLAN INCLUDES

Universal guarantees, investment and a transition for work

Healthcare

A universal single-payer Medicare for All system, with a supplemental private market for elective or additional services.

Families & education

Universal child care and pre-K; tuition-free public colleges, trade schools and technical programs; and forgiveness of outstanding federal student-loan debt.

Income & employment

An illustrative $300-per-month adult universal-income benefit combined with a countercyclical federal employment backstop that expands when private employment weakens.

Housing & infrastructure

Large, phased investment intended to expand housing supply, infrastructure and productive capacity rather than relying primarily on demand subsidies.

Retirement

A stronger universal Social Security floor tied to basic living needs, with earlier eligibility considered for physically demanding occupations.

Workers & technology

Stronger unions and worker participation, broader retirement and ownership opportunities, and a gradual shorter-workweek transition as productivity permits.

ILLUSTRATIVE FINANCING

How the model attempts to pay for the expansion

For purposes of testing the program—not as finalized policy rates—the model uses an employer healthcare contribution around 6 percent of payroll and a household healthcare contribution around 3 percent of income above a protected threshold. Those payments are modeled partly as replacements for private insurance premiums rather than simply as new costs layered on top of existing premiums.

The scenario also tests a combined two-percentage-point increase in Social Security payroll contributions, phased removal of the Social Security taxable-earnings ceiling for high earnings, a corporate tax rate around 28 percent and additional taxation of very high incomes and capital income.

The model further assumes a narrowly defined AI-related levy that grows with the AI economy, a public AI wealth fund, additional revenue measures focused on finance and very high wealth, and a roughly 15–20 percent phased reduction in defense spending while retaining national-defense capacity.

These rates are placeholders used to determine whether the larger policy framework can be made fiscally coherent. They are not presented as final tax legislation.

THE AI GROWTH ASSUMPTION

An intentionally optimistic productivity scenario

The model assumes AI eventually contributes roughly 0.7 percentage point per year to total-factor-productivity growth as adoption spreads through the economy. This is an optimistic assumption, not a forecast. In February 2026, Federal Reserve Governor Michael Barr cited research estimating that AI could add roughly 0.3 to 0.9 percentage point to annual total-factor-productivity growth over the coming decade, while emphasizing substantial uncertainty about adoption and economic effects.

Under the scenario, AI-related growth is phased in rather than appearing immediately. The resulting larger economy produces additional tax revenue without requiring every dollar of new fiscal capacity to come from higher statutory tax rates.

Research context: See the Federal Reserve Board's February 17, 2026 discussion of AI and productivity: Artificial intelligence and the labor market.

THE FISCAL TEST

Large government, but not an assumption of unlimited borrowing

The starting fiscal environment is difficult even before these proposals are considered. The Congressional Budget Office's February 2026 current-law baseline projects a $3.1 trillion federal deficit in 2036, equal to 6.7 percent of GDP, with debt held by the public reaching 120 percent of GDP.

The purpose of this model is therefore not simply to add programs to the existing budget. It tests a larger tax-and-transfer system in which major new benefits are paired with new permanent revenue and in which some federal spending replaces costs currently paid privately, particularly health-insurance premiums.

In the earlier high-AI version of this model, the illustrative economy reached roughly $50–51 trillion of nominal GDP by 2036, with federal revenue around $12 trillion, spending around $15 trillion and a deficit around $3 trillion. The resulting debt ratio was modeled near 111 percent of GDP—below the current-law CBO projection for 2036—but that result depends heavily on the assumed productivity growth, tax structure, program costs and interest rates.

Extending the same framework through 2040 is intended as a planning scenario rather than a point forecast. The objective would be to keep deficits near or below the share of GDP in the current-law baseline while allowing economic growth to outpace growth in public debt.

Baseline context: CBO projects the current-law federal deficit rising from $1.9 trillion in 2026 to $3.1 trillion in 2036 and debt held by the public rising from 101 percent to 120 percent of GDP. CBO also stresses that budget outcomes become increasingly uncertain as projections extend further into the future. See The Budget and Economic Outlook: 2026 to 2036.

2030–2040 PATH

Build capacity first, then share more of the productivity dividend

Early 2030s: Phase in healthcare financing and coverage, child care and education guarantees, housing and infrastructure construction, workforce training, the employment backstop and the initial AI wealth fund. Keep the universal-income benefit modest while productive capacity is being expanded.

Middle of the decade: As housing, energy, healthcare and workforce capacity expand, continue the Social Security transition and allow AI-related public revenue and wealth-fund assets to accumulate. Use measured productivity rather than a calendar promise to guide reductions in standard working hours.

Late 2030s: If the assumed productivity gains have actually materialized and inflation remains controlled, a larger portion of the productivity dividend could be directed toward shorter working time, public-wealth accumulation, deficit reduction or future increases in the universal dividend.

If productivity disappoints, the model does not assume that government can spend the missing output into existence. Expansion of productivity-linked benefits would slow while the permanent guarantees would rely on their dedicated financing.

WHAT WOULD HAVE TO GO RIGHT

The model's major economic dependencies

The scenario works best if AI and automation generate substantial real productivity growth; housing construction keeps pace with increased purchasing power; healthcare and child-care capacity expand; technical education supplies workers needed for construction, energy and infrastructure; tax revenues broadly perform as modeled; and interest costs do not rise enough to overwhelm the growth dividend.

The principal downside risk is the opposite combination: strong demand from new benefits without sufficient growth in housing, healthcare, labor, energy and other real productive capacity. That could produce higher inflation, higher interest rates and faster debt accumulation. CBO notes that economic conditions—including productivity, income, inflation and interest rates—can materially alter federal revenues, spending and deficits.

Modeling context: CBO's April 2026 analysis illustrates how changes in economic conditions can alter federal budget outcomes: How Changes in Economic Conditions Might Affect the Federal Budget: 2026 to 2036.

HOW TO READ THIS PLAN

A framework to test, revise and measure

This 2030–2040 plan is an illustrative synthesis of Yagudayev's developing policy positions. It is not a Congressional Budget Office score, Federal Reserve forecast or independent prediction of future economic performance. Dollar amounts, tax rates, growth assumptions and fiscal outcomes should be updated as policies become more specific and as economic evidence changes.

The underlying test is straightforward: whether a broader system of universal guarantees and public investment can be financed while expanding America's real productive capacity quickly enough that incomes and output grow faster than the public-debt burden.
