2030–2040 HIGH-PRODUCTIVITY ECONOMIC MODEL

When We Win! Project Democratic Socialism: 10 Year Plan for America.

2030–2040

This plan brings Boris Yagudayev's economic and social-policy proposals together around a deliberate central premise: that artificial intelligence, automation and complementary investment can produce a major productivity expansion during the 2030s, and that public policy can help translate part of that expanding productive capacity into broader economic security, public investment and shorter working time.

The title reflects Yagudayev's framing rather than an election forecast. The figures are an illustrative high-growth scenario, not a CBO or Federal Reserve forecast.

THE IDEA

Build a larger productive economy—and broaden participation in its gains

The model combines universal healthcare, child care and pre-K, tuition-free public higher education and technical training, a stronger Social Security floor, a modest universal income, an employment backstop, large-scale housing and infrastructure investment, stronger worker participation and a gradual move toward a shorter workweek.

These programs are modeled with dedicated healthcare and Social Security contributions, more progressive individual and corporate taxation, taxation of capital income, savings from a phased reduction in defense spending and new revenue connected to the AI economy.

The central economic bet is high productivity growth. The model assumes that AI and automation increasingly complement workers, accelerate innovation and expand American output as adoption spreads through businesses and public institutions. A public AI wealth fund and AI-related revenue mechanisms are intended to give the public a financial stake in part of that expansion.

Housing, healthcare capacity, energy, infrastructure and skilled labor are expanded alongside household purchasing power so that the plan's social guarantees are paired with growth in the real goods and services available to Americans.

WHAT IT COULD MEAN FOR EVERYDAY PEOPLE

A larger economy should translate into more room in the household budget

The central household goal of the plan is straightforward: economic growth should leave ordinary people with more purchasing power after taxes and major expenses—not simply produce a larger GDP number. In the plan's high-AI-productivity scenario, real market incomes rise while several large household costs are reduced or shifted into universal public systems.

Higher real incomes

The model assumes AI, automation and complementary investment raise productivity and real market incomes over time. For a middle-income household, the illustrative scenario raises market income from roughly $74,500 today to about $94,600 in today's purchasing power by the mature stage of the plan.

A basic cash floor

The model includes an illustrative $300-per-month universal benefit for each adult. A two-adult household would receive $7,200 per year, adding a predictable income floor on top of wages or other income.

Less paid out privately

Medicare for All, expanded housing supply, public education and child-care policies are intended to reduce what households must pay directly for healthcare, housing, education and care. Some of those private costs would be replaced by taxes, so the relevant comparison is the total household budget—not taxes alone.

More financial room

In the illustrative distributional model, the combination of higher real income, the universal benefit and lower effective household costs increases resources remaining after modeled taxes and a comparable spending basket across the income distribution. The size of the gain varies substantially by income and household circumstances.

A middle-income example

For the middle 20 percent of households, the scenario models about $101,800 in gross resources after the productivity-driven income increase and a two-adult universal benefit. After illustrative taxes, roughly $90,400 remains. The model estimates that a spending basket comparable to today's roughly $66,900 middle-quintile basket would have an effective household cost around $57,500, leaving roughly $32,900 a year—or about $2,740 a month—for additional consumption, saving, investing or debt repayment.

What “better off” means in this model

It does not mean every household receives the same benefit or pays less tax. Higher-income households carry a larger financing burden. The modeled improvement comes from looking at the whole balance sheet: real income, cash benefits, taxes and the private costs of necessities together. Under the central assumptions, the model produces higher household resources across income groups while preserving substantial differences in income.

Important: These are illustrative scenario results, not promises or an independent government score. They depend especially on strong AI-driven productivity growth, successful expansion of housing and other real productive capacity, the eventual tax design and how productivity gains are divided between workers and capital. BLS's 2024 Consumer Expenditure Survey provides the household-spending benchmark; the household outcomes above are Yagudayev-plan modeling assumptions.

Household-data context: BLS reports average 2024 annual expenditures of $35,046, $50,054, $66,900, $89,972 and $150,342 from the lowest through highest income quintiles, respectively. See Consumer Expenditures—2024. CBO's distributional work similarly evaluates household resources by combining market income, transfers and federal taxes; see The Distribution of Household Income, 2022.

WHAT THE PLAN INCLUDES

Universal guarantees, investment and a transition for work

Healthcare

A universal single-payer Medicare for All system, with a supplemental private market for elective or additional services.

Families & education

Universal child care and pre-K; tuition-free public colleges, trade schools and technical programs; and forgiveness of outstanding federal student-loan debt.

Income & employment

An illustrative $300-per-month adult universal-income benefit combined with a countercyclical federal employment backstop that expands when private employment weakens.

Housing & infrastructure

Large, phased investment intended to expand housing supply, infrastructure and productive capacity alongside increased household purchasing power.

Retirement

A stronger universal Social Security floor tied to basic living needs, with earlier eligibility considered for physically demanding occupations.

Workers & technology

Stronger unions and worker participation, broader retirement and ownership opportunities, and a gradual shorter-workweek transition as productivity rises.

ILLUSTRATIVE FINANCING

Financing the expansion while growing the tax base

For purposes of testing the program—not as finalized policy rates—the model uses an employer healthcare contribution around 6 percent of payroll and a household healthcare contribution around 3 percent of income above a protected threshold. Those payments are modeled partly as replacements for private insurance premiums rather than simply as new costs layered on top of existing premiums.

The scenario also tests a combined two-percentage-point increase in Social Security payroll contributions, phased removal of the Social Security taxable-earnings ceiling for high earnings, a corporate tax rate around 28 percent and additional taxation of very high incomes and capital income.

The model further assumes a narrowly defined AI-related levy that grows with the AI economy, a public AI wealth fund, additional revenue measures focused on finance and very high wealth, and a roughly 15–20 percent phased reduction in defense spending while retaining national-defense capacity.

A central feature of the model is that stronger productivity also enlarges the tax base. By the mature years, part of the additional fiscal capacity comes from a larger economy rather than exclusively from higher statutory tax rates.

THE AI GROWTH PREMISE

Betting on the high-productivity side of a credible research range

This model deliberately adopts a high-productivity AI scenario. It assumes AI eventually contributes roughly 0.7 percentage point per year to total-factor-productivity growth as adoption spreads through the economy. That assumption sits toward the upper portion of a research range cited in February 2026 by Federal Reserve Governor Michael Barr: one study estimated that AI could add roughly 0.3 to 0.9 percentage point to annual total-factor-productivity growth over the coming decade.

Barr described AI as very likely to have a profound positive impact on long-run productivity and noted growing evidence that AI assistants can improve efficiency, speed and accuracy at individual tasks. He also noted that AI could accelerate research and development itself, potentially increasing the rate of innovation. The model therefore treats strong AI-driven productivity as its central scenario while recognizing that the timing and magnitude cannot be known in advance.

Research context: Federal Reserve Governor Michael Barr, February 17, 2026: What Will Artificial Intelligence Mean for the Labor Market and the Economy?

THE FISCAL TEST

Use growth and dedicated financing to keep debt from outrunning the economy

The United States enters this scenario with a challenging current-law fiscal outlook. CBO's February 2026 baseline projects a $3.1 trillion federal deficit in 2036, equal to 6.7 percent of GDP, with debt held by the public reaching 120 percent of GDP.

This model does not simply add new programs to that baseline. It pairs major permanent benefits with new permanent revenue, replaces some costs currently paid privately—particularly health-insurance premiums—with public financing, and assumes stronger productivity enlarges national output and the federal tax base.

In the model, nominal GDP reaches roughly $50.7 trillion in 2036, federal revenue about $12 trillion, spending about $15 trillion and the deficit about $3 trillion. Debt held by the public is modeled near 111 percent of GDP at that point, compared with CBO's 120 percent current-law projection for 2036.

The 2030–2040 extension below carries the same high-productivity framework forward. It illustrates a federal government financing substantially broader social guarantees while attempting to keep economic output growing at least as fast as the public-debt burden.

Baseline context: CBO projects the current-law federal deficit rising from $1.9 trillion in 2026 to $3.1 trillion in 2036 and debt held by the public rising from 101 percent to 120 percent of GDP. See The Budget and Economic Outlook: 2026 to 2036.

2030–2040 PATH

Build capacity, raise productivity and broaden the dividend

2030–31

Build the foundation

Begin healthcare financing, child care and education guarantees, housing and infrastructure construction, workforce training and the AI wealth fund.

2032–34

Expand capacity

Scale housing, healthcare, energy and skilled-labor capacity as AI adoption and complementary investment deepen.

2035–37

Broaden the dividend

Public AI-related revenue and wealth-fund assets grow while Social Security and worker-participation reforms mature.

2038–40

Convert productivity into time & security

Under the high-productivity scenario, more of the dividend can support shorter working time, public wealth, household security and fiscal stabilization.

Early 2030s: Phase in healthcare financing and coverage, child care and education guarantees, housing and infrastructure construction, workforce training, the employment backstop and the initial AI wealth fund. The emphasis is on building productive capacity at the same time the social guarantees expand.

Middle of the decade: Continue the Social Security transition while housing, energy, healthcare and workforce capacity expand. AI-related public revenue and wealth-fund assets accumulate as AI adoption deepens across the economy.

Late 2030s: Under the model's central high-productivity assumption, a growing share of the productivity dividend can support shorter working time, public-wealth accumulation, deficit reduction and potentially larger future social dividends.

The shorter-workweek transition is tied to productivity rather than an assumption that fewer hours automatically produce the same output. The objective is for technological progress to translate into a combination of greater national output, higher material living standards and more personal time.

DISTRIBUTION & OWNERSHIP

Broaden who participates in economic and asset growth

Wealth is substantially more concentrated than income. As a historical benchmark, CBO estimates that the top 10 percent of U.S. families held 60 percent of family wealth in 2022, while the bottom half held 6 percent. The plan does not yet contain enough modeled household balance-sheet data to responsibly claim a specific 2040 wealth distribution. Instead, the second visual shows the mechanisms through which the plan is intended to broaden participation in future gains.

Wealth-data context: Congressional Budget Office, Trends in the Distribution of Family Wealth, 1989 to 2022. CBO reports that the top 10 percent held 60 percent of wealth in 2022 and the bottom half held 6 percent.

THE ECONOMIC MECHANISM

Growth is the engine of the model

The plan treats housing construction, infrastructure, child care, education, workforce development and AI investment as complementary pieces. More child-care capacity can expand labor-force participation; technical education can supply workers for housing, energy and infrastructure; additional housing can absorb stronger household demand; and AI can raise output per hour. The model's fiscal logic depends on those real-economy gains feeding a larger tax base.

The main uncertainty is the pace at which those gains arrive. If productivity or real productive capacity grows more slowly than assumed, fiscal outcomes would differ and inflation or interest costs could be higher. That is a sensitivity of the model rather than its central case.

MODEL ASSUMPTIONS & SOURCES

What drives the numbers

The central case assumes AI's contribution to total-factor-productivity growth eventually reaches about 0.7 percentage point annually; major social programs are phased in with dedicated financing; the illustrative corporate rate reaches about 28 percent; healthcare contributions replace part of existing private premium spending; Social Security financing is strengthened; defense spending is reduced gradually; and housing, infrastructure and workforce capacity expand alongside demand.

Federal Reserve Governor Michael Barr cited research estimating an AI contribution of 0.3–0.9 percentage point to annual total-factor-productivity growth over the coming decade. CBO's February 2026 current-law baseline provides the comparison for federal deficits, revenue, spending and debt. The model's policy figures and 2030–2040 outputs are Yagudayev-plan scenario assumptions and are not estimates produced or endorsed by either institution.
